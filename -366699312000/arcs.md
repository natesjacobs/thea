# Arcs: from cultivation to states, or not

Seven regions. Dates in years BP (before 1950); BCE dates converted by adding 1,950. Values are from abstracts unless marked. Every row has a link. Lags are arithmetic on the dates shown, and the choice of start point is ours.

## Summary

| Region | First cultivation | Intensive or staple farming | First state | Lag from first cultivation | Lag from intensive farming |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Southwest Asia | 11,600 | ~8,450 (irrigation villages on the alluvium) | ~5,450–5,050 | ~6,300 | ~3,200 |
| Egypt | ~7,350 (crops arrive) | ~6,500 | ~5,050 | ~2,300 | ~1,500 |
| North China | ~10,300 | not fixed | ~3,700 | ~6,600 | — |
| Lower Yangtze | — | 6,900–6,600 (rice domesticated) | ~5,250 (contested) | — | ~1,500 |
| Mesoamerica | 8,700 | 4,000 | ~2,250–2,050 | ~6,500 | ~1,850 |
| Andes | ~10,000 | ~4,950 (monument centres) | ~2,100 | ~7,900 | ~2,850 |
| Indus | ~7,150–6,850 (crops arrive) | ~5,000–4,500 | cities ~4,550; state contested | ~2,450 to cities | ~500 to cities |
| New Guinea | 10,220–9,910 | 6,950–6,440 | none | — | — |

Two readings. From first cultivation, the independent centres took 6,000 to 8,000 years. From intensive farming, 1,500 to 3,200. A-003's ~3,000 years sits at the top of the second range. Egypt and the Indus borrowed crops already domesticated in Southwest Asia, so their local clocks start late and run short; counted from the first cultivation anywhere, both are slower than Mesopotamia. New Guinea farmed for ten thousand years and built no state.

## Indus

Crops and herd animals came from the west, already domesticated. Zebu cattle were domesticated locally.

| BP | Event | Source |
|---|---|---|
| ~7,150–6,850 | Aceramic Neolithic at Mehrgarh begins, 5200–4900 BCE; lasts 186–531 years. Settlers brought domesticated plants and animals | [Mutin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92621-5) (full text read) |
| — | The older chronology put Mehrgarh at ~8000 BCE and treated it as a local origin. Mutin et al. reject it; the debate may not be over | same; [Jarrige's view summarized there](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000376) |
| ~6,600 | Pottery at Mehrgarh, no earlier than ~4650 BCE | [Mutin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92621-5) |
| ~5,250 | Pre-Urban phase: permanent settlements in each region, ca. 3300 BCE | [Green 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10814-020-09147-9) (open access) |
| ~5,000–4,500 | Rivers stabilize as the monsoon weakens; this probably stimulated intensive farming, then cities around 4,500 BP | [Giosan et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112743109) |
| ~4,550–3,850 | Urban phase, ca. 2600–1900 BCE. Planned cities; no palaces, large temples, rich tombs or monuments to rulers | [Green 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10814-020-09147-9) |
| from ~3,900 | Settlements shrink and shift east as the monsoon declines further | [Giosan et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1112743109) |
| ~3,850–3,250 | Post-Urban phase, ca. 1900–1300 BCE | [Green 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10814-020-09147-9) |

Whether the Indus was a state is unresolved. Green finds no clear ruling class after nearly a century of digging and concludes that a ruling class is not a prerequisite for social complexity. He does not rule on statehood. If it was not a state, it is the case where farming reached cities without producing a Goliath.
