# -142009198232774400

**−4.50 Gyr.** Theia strikes the proto-Earth. The Moon forms.

About 70 Myr after the first solids. The date is contested, from ~4.51 to ~4.35 Ga. Recent work favors an old Moon and reads the ~4.35 Ga ages as a later remelting (Nimmo, Kleine & Morbidelli 2024). This anchor takes 4.50 Ga.

A giant impact is consensus. Which impact is not. Every model has to explain three things. The Moon is short of iron, with a core only 1–1.5% of its mass. It is short of volatiles: K/U is ~2,500 against Earth's ~12,000. And it is isotopically identical to Earth. Oxygen matches to 0.2 ± 1.6 ppm (Fischer et al. 2024). Titanium, chromium, calcium and iron match too. Across meteorites, oxygen varies by thousands of ppm.

## The models

- **Canonical** (Canup & Asphaug 2001). Mars-mass impactor, 0.10–0.15 Earth masses, ~45°, near escape speed. Gets the iron and angular momentum right. But 70–80% of the disk is Theia, so the Moon should differ from Earth. It doesn't.
- **Fast-spinning Earth** (Ćuk & Stewart 2012). Earth already spins in ~2.5 hr. A small, fast hit splashes out Earth's own mantle. The extra spin is drained later through the evection resonance. Whether the drain works is contested.
- **Half-Earth** (Canup 2012). Two near-equal bodies. Both end up the same mixture, so the isotopes match automatically. Leaves too much angular momentum.
- **Synestia** (Lock & Stewart 2017, 2018). The impact is energetic enough that Earth stops being a planet. It becomes a spinning doughnut of rock vapor. The Moon grows inside it and equilibrates with it. Explains the isotopes and the volatile loss.
- **Many impacts** (Rufu et al. 2017). Twenty or so smaller hits, each making a moonlet. The moonlets merge over tens of Myr. Averaging dilutes any one impactor.
- **Immediate satellite** (Kegerreis et al. 2022; Denton et al. 2026). At high resolution, or with real rock strength, a Moon-mass body is thrown straight into orbit within hours. Denton: a hot but solid Theia, ~2,000 K at the surface, leaves an intact remnant of 1.5–1.8 lunar masses. A molten Theia leaves a disk. Popular coverage reported this backwards.

**Where it stands.** No model wins. The newest isotope data (Hopp et al. 2025) point to Theia being already Earth-like: born in the inner Solar System, from the same material, possibly slightly closer to the Sun. That rescues the canonical impact without needing heavy mixing. It is the simplest reading and this anchor follows it.

## Theia

- Mass ~0.13 Earth masses. Radius ~3,500 km, about Mars. Differentiated: an iron core ~30% of its mass under an FeO-rich mantle.
- Formed in the inner disk, near 1 AU. Inner-system origin is the current lead. A carbonaceous, outer-system Theia is contested.
- **Speculative:** Belbruno & Gott 2005. Theia grew at Earth's L4 or L5 point, 60° ahead or behind in the same orbit, and later drifted out. That would explain both the shared composition and the low impact speed. It is not a standard simulation outcome.

## The sequence

Best estimate. Numbers from the papers cited, ranges where they disagree. Lines marked *estimate* are order-of-magnitude arithmetic, not published.

### The long approach

The gas disk is gone by ~5 Myr. Without gas drag, the inner system's 30–40 embryos stir each other. Orbits stretch and cross. Close passes become common. Few end in collision: a hit needs a pass within a few Earth radii, inside a Hill sphere ~200 Earth radii across.

Theia and the proto-Earth pass and repass for tens of Myr. Each encounter alters the next. Leftover planetesimals damp the relative speed to a few km/s. In simulations the last giant impact falls at 20–70 Myr in most runs, and as late as 150 Myr.

In the co-orbital version, Theia sits at L4 for tens of Myr, is nudged into a horseshoe orbit, then a chaotic creeping one, then falls in at near zero energy.

### The last day

- Theia enters Earth's Hill sphere, ~1.5 million km out, a day or more before contact. *Estimate.*
- It accelerates from a few km/s to ~9–10 km/s, the mutual escape speed.
- At 50,000 km it spans ~4° of Earth's sky, eight full Moons. At contact it fills the sky. *Estimate.*
- It crosses the Roche limit, ~18,500 km, about 30–40 minutes out. Too fast to break up. In the last minutes both bodies bulge toward each other by kilometres to tens of kilometres. *Estimate.*

### Impact

- **0 min.** Contact at ~45°, the most probable angle for any impact. ~9.5 km/s.
- **0–10 min.** Shock waves of tens to hundreds of GPa cross both mantles. Total energy ~4–10 × 10³¹ J, about ten billion Chicxulubs. *Estimate.* The contact zone vaporizes. The hottest material passes 10⁴ K. *Estimate.*
- **10 min–1 hr.** Theia shears across Earth's face. Its mantle smears into a long arm. Its iron core, denser and more compact, ploughs into Earth's mantle.
- **1–5 hr.** The arm wraps around the planet. Gravity from the distorted, spinning mass torques material into orbit. Part of Theia comes back and hits a second time. In the hot-Theia case, a coherent body separates and stays in orbit at ~5 hr.
- **Hours to a day.** Theia's core sinks through the molten mantle and joins Earth's. Almost none of it reaches orbit, which is why the Moon is short of iron.
- At least the upper half of Earth's mantle melts.
- About 2% of Earth's mass in Theia mantle may reach the solid lower mantle. If it was 2–3.5% denser, it sinks and piles at the core-mantle boundary. Those piles may be the two LLVPs (large low-velocity provinces) under Africa and the Pacific today (Yuan et al. 2023). Contested.

### The first centuries

- Earth spins once every ~5 hr. In high-angular-momentum models, 2–3 hr. The equator bulges.
- In orbit: 1.3–3 lunar masses of debris. In the canonical case it is 10–20% vapor. In a synestia, 80–90%, and there is no clear edge between planet and disk.
- Earth sits inside an atmosphere of rock vapor: SiO, Na, K, Mg, Fe, thousands of kelvin. The photosphere sits where silicate condenses, ~2,000–2,300 K. Molten rock rains back out.
- Silicate vapor is too heavy to escape. The system is close to closed.
- Volatiles leave slowly. K and Rb isotopes say the vapor was 98–99% saturated as it lost them (Nie & Dauphas 2019). A slow leak, not a blow-off.
- From above the clouds: a glowing, flattened, orange-white body, turning visibly in a few hours. There is no surface to stand on. *Estimate.*

### The Moon assembles

The Roche limit for lunar rock is 2.9 Earth radii, ~18,500 km. Inside it, tides pull clumps apart. The Moon must form outside it. Best estimate: born at 3–6 Earth radii, 19,000–38,000 km. Today it is at 60.

How fast depends on the model.

| Model | Time | How |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate satellite | Hours | Intact clump thrown into orbit |
| Disk (Salmon & Canup 2012) | Months for 40%; ~100 yr for the rest | Outer disk clumps fast; inner disk spreads out and feeds moonlets |
| Synestia | Years to decades | Grows inside tens of bars of rock vapor at 3,000–4,000 K |
| Many impacts | Tens of Myr | Moonlets merge |

Composite: most of the mass is in place within 1–100 years. The surface is molten throughout.

From Earth, through breaks in the cloud: a red-orange Moon, ~10° across, twenty times its present width. It crosses the sky in hours. Its orbital period is ~10–15 hr. *Estimate.*

### The magma ocean

Consensus that it existed. Details contested. Standard sequence (Elkins-Tanton et al. 2011):

- Molten to ~1,000 km depth. Possibly the whole Moon.
- About 80% solid within ~1,000 years. Olivine and pyroxene crystallize and sink. They become the mantle.
- When ~100 km of liquid remains, plagioclase appears. It is lighter than the melt. It floats. It builds the pale highland crust, 40–50 km thick.
- The crust insulates. The last melt lingers for tens of Myr. Rb–Sr dating puts it near-solid ~70 Myr after formation.
- The last 1–2% of liquid, trapped between crust and mantle, collects what didn't fit into crystals: potassium, rare earths, phosphorus, uranium, thorium. This is KREEP. Its heat sits mostly under the nearside and later feeds the dark maria.
- Dense cumulates near the top later overturn and sink.

From Earth, the glow fades as the crust closes over. The Moon turns grey.

### Recession

- Tidal push falls off steeply with distance. Recession was violent at first. The Moon likely moved from ~5 to beyond ~10–20 Earth radii within 10⁴–10⁶ years. *Estimate.*
- Tides on Earth at 4 Earth radii: ~3,400 times today's. *Estimate.*
- A magma-ocean Earth dissipates tides very differently from an ocean Earth. Rates in this period are poorly known.
- On the way out the Moon passes through the evection resonance. Angular momentum may have been drained there.
- ~4.35 Ga: tidal heating, set by the orbit's evolution, may have remelted the Moon and reset its rock ages (Nimmo et al. 2024).
- By the Hadean, ~30–40 Earth radii. 1.5–2° across. Day 10–15 hr. *Estimate.*
- Today: 38.30 ± 0.09 mm/yr (lunar laser ranging, 1970–2015).

## The size of the Moon

The Moon is 1.23% of Earth's mass, 1/81. Its radius is 27% of Earth's.

| Satellite / planet | Mass ratio |
|---|---|
| Charon / Pluto | ~0.12 |
| Moon / Earth | 0.0123 |
| Triton / Neptune | ~2 × 10⁻⁴ |
| Titan / Saturn | ~2.4 × 10⁻⁴ |
| Ganymede / Jupiter | ~8 × 10⁻⁵ |
| Phobos / Mars | ~2 × 10⁻⁸ |

- Only Charon is larger relative to its planet. Pluto and Charon orbit a point in space between them.
- Giant-planet moons formed in gas disks. Those seem to cap total moon mass near 10⁻⁴ of the planet. A Moon at 10⁻² takes a giant impact.
- Big moons may only form around planets smaller than ~1.6 Earth radii. Larger rocky planets make all-vapor disks that fail to build one (Nakajima et al. 2022).
- Estimated frequency: about 1 in 12 terrestrial planets. Range 1 in 45 to 1 in 4 (Elser et al. 2011). No exomoon is confirmed.
- The Moon holds Earth's tilt to 22.1–24.5°. Without it, early models said 0–85° (Laskar et al. 1993). Later integrations say smaller and slower swings, enough to allow habitability (Lissauer et al. 2012). Contested.

## The eclipse coincidence

The Sun is ~400 times wider than the Moon. It is ~390 times farther away. Both cover about half a degree.

- Moon: 29.4′ at apogee, 33.5′ at perigee. Sun: 31.5′ to 32.5′. The Moon's range straddles the Sun's. So there are both total eclipses and annular ones. Annulars are already more common: 33% of solar eclipses against 27% total, 2000 BCE–3000 CE.
- The coincidence is temporary. The Moon recedes ~3.8 cm a year. The Sun slowly swells.
- At birth the Moon was ~20 times wider than the Sun. Every eclipse was total and blotted out the corona with it.
- Annular eclipses began only when the far Moon became smaller than the near Sun. Roughly 1–2 Gyr ago. *Estimate.*
- The last total eclipse: ~600 Myr from now (Vondrak). Meeus: intermittent from ~620 Myr, the very last at ~1.2 Gyr. Range across estimates 250 Myr–1.2 Gyr.
- No known physics links the two. The Moon's size comes from a single random impact. Its distance comes from 4.5 Gyr of tidal friction set by Earth's oceans. Consensus: chance.

### Uses

- **1868. Helium.** Janssen, in India, sees a yellow line in prominences during the eclipse. Lockyer and Frankland name it for the Sun. It is found on Earth in 1895. The first element found in space before Earth.
- **1869. The corona.** A green line at 530.3 nm, assigned to a new element, "coronium." In 1939–42 Grotrian and Edlén show it is iron missing 13 electrons. The corona is at a million kelvin. Nobody yet knows why it is so much hotter than the surface below it.
- **1919. Relativity.** Dyson, Eddington and Davidson measure starlight bending at the Sun's edge. Einstein predicts 1.75″. Sobral gives 1.98 ± 0.12″, Príncipe 1.61 ± 0.30″. Repeated in 2017: 1.752″, to ~3%.

### Musings

- **Privileged Planet** (Gonzalez & Richards 2004). They argue that the best place for life is also the best place to observe, and read design into it. Mainstream astronomy rejects it. Total eclipses are a passing phase. Coronagraphs have done the same job since 1930. The argument picks which conveniences count.
- **Selection.** Corona-revealing eclipses span a window of order 1–2 Gyr out of a ~5 Gyr Earth–Moon history, so observers landing inside it is not improbable. A big moon may help habitability a little, through tilt and tides. That would be a weak link between having observers and having a big moon. Speculative.
- **Bracketing.** Animal life began ~540 Myr ago. The last total eclipse is ~600 Myr ahead. Total eclipses that show the corona span roughly the age of animals, give or take. Chance. Noted.
- **For this timeline.** The last total eclipse comes well before the CO₂ endpoint (`../41024881767225600`, +1.3 Gyr). The last plants on land see only annulars. Nothing alive then will have seen the corona.
- **The title.** The impactor named Thea left behind the one body in the sky that can exactly cover the Sun. For a while.
