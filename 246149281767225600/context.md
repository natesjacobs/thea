**+7.8 Gyr from now.** The planetary nebula. The Sun stops being a star.

Core helium is spent. What remains is a degenerate carbon-oxygen core with two shells burning above it, hydrogen and helium, neither stable. The helium shell ignites in pulses a few hundred years long, each one convulsing the envelope. Mass pours off at 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴ solar masses a year.

Then the envelope leaves entirely.

What it exposes is the core: Earth-sized, above 100,000 K, radiating several thousand L☉ almost entirely in the ultraviolet. That radiation ionizes the departing gas from the inside and the shell lights up — oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, each in its own emission lines, structured by the thermal pulses that drove it off. Shells inside shells, the record of the Sun's last convulsions written in glowing gas.

It lasts about ten thousand years. Then the shell thins past visibility and disperses into the interstellar medium, carrying the carbon and nitrogen and s-process metals the Sun dredged up from its own interior. Those atoms go on to be part of something else.

What's left behind generates nothing. Every photon it emits from here on is leftover heat.

The name is a misnomer from the 18th century — the round ones looked like planets through early telescopes. There are no planets in it.

Immediately after this the remnant is at peak output as a white dwarf: ~15 L☉, ~5% of today's sunlight in the visible, lethal in the ultraviolet. Anything once at 1 AU is now near 2 AU, the Sun having shed half its mass.
