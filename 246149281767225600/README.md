**+7.8 Gyr from now.** Peak white dwarf intensity.

What is left is a carbon-oxygen core of ~0.5 solar masses, roughly Earth-sized, at a surface temperature above 100,000 K. It generates nothing. Every photon it emits is residual heat from the star it used to be, leaking out through a thin insulating skin.

At its brightest it puts out about 15 L☉. A planet still at 1 AU would take fifteen times Earth's current energy flux.

But the light is wrong. A 100,000 K surface peaks near 30 nm, in the extreme UV and soft X-ray. Only a sliver of the output is visible. In visible light it delivers about 5% of today's sunlight — roughly what Jupiter gets, or a heavily overcast afternoon. You could read by it.

It is also about 25 arcseconds across. Too small for an eye to resolve. Not a disk, a point: an intensely bright blue-white star you cannot look at, casting hard-edged shadows across a dim landscape.

And it is lethal. The invisible majority of that output ionizes atmospheres, strips them, and sterilizes anything exposed. A gentle-looking light over a blowtorch.

Two adjustments worth holding onto:

- Just before this, during the planetary nebula phase, the exposed core briefly runs at several thousand L☉ for maybe ten thousand years. That is the real maximum, if you want one.
- Any planet that was at 1 AU has drifted out to roughly 2 AU as the Sun shed half its mass. Four times the area, so a quarter of everything above.

Then it fades. Ten billion years later it is a 4,000 K ember at 0.00004 L☉.
